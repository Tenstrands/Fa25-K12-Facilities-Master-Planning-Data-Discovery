1. Structure and content of plans
2. Detailed plan assessment

1. Facility inventory and condition assessment

3. Educational alignment

The largest part of this project is a detailed evaluation of key plan components using a 1-4 scoring scale. You will provide scores on the following components: 
1. Facility inventory and condition assessment
2. Long-term planning for enrollment and capacity utilization
3. Educational alignment
4. Outdoor spaces and campus greening
5. Climate risk and mitigation strategies
6. Energy efficiency and  resilience
7. Budget and funding strategy
Scoring rubric overview


We will score each element on a scale from 1-4 that measures the depth and detail for a given element. We will refine this rubric during the first few weeks of the project, based on your initial findings. Our goal is to capture the quality of planning and identify model plans. 


Level
	Description
	1 -📕
	No mention / cursory mention.
	2 - 📙
	Includes a limited description (analysis, discussion, considerations, strategies, etc.), with limited data and planning.
	3 -📗
	Includes sufficient data and appropriate level of detail for strategic planning (analysis, discussion, considerations, strategies, etc., but missing some key strategies or indicators.
	4 -📘
	Includes significant detail and description (analysis, discussion, considerations, strategies, etc.) that is backed with district-wide data, model plan element. The component is well-developed enough to provide the district with realistic guidance and a reference for evaluating how the district is complying with the plan.

1. Facility inventory and condition assessment


Facility Inventory & Condition Assessment
	This component has two parts, and plans should be evaluated on both.


(1) All plans should include an inventory of all district facilities—including schools, administrative buildings, and other properties. A facility inventory should have sufficient detail to analyze how facilities are meeting district needs. It should include information such as:
* Building age, size, and type (including portables)
* Details about classrooms (square footage, characteristics)
* Specialized spaces (science labs, CTE facilities, athletic fields, multipurpose rooms, cafeterias, libraries, etc.). 


(2) In addition, plans should include a detailed assessment of facility conditions. The condition assessment should be more than just identifying big problems (i.e. leaking roofs and cracked windows). Plans should include detailed assessments of facility quality and status that can inform long-term planning. The plan should include a description of the assessment tool used (e.g. a scoring system).
	1 -📕
	No mention or minimal reference: Inventory does not include all sites in the district (may include only some schools).  There is not information about building age, size, systems, or major features. 


Assessments only include significant deficiencies rather than an assessment score for each site. 


Plan does not acknowledge condition-related impacts on student learning (e.g., air quality, temperature, safety).
	2 - 📙
	Limited description: Inventory includes a basic list of all facilities in the district (e.g., number of schools and portable classrooms). Plan does not include details such as square footage of classrooms and other spaces or detailed site plans. 


Assessment includes all district sites, but uses a simple assessment tool such as the Facility Inspection Tool (FIT). 


Plan does not provide details on building systems (HVAC, lighting, plumbing) or non-classroom spaces. This means it may mention those components but does not provide any information about their condition or potential need for repair.
	3 -📗
	Sufficient detail: Inventory includes all facilities with some detail, including building sizes and ages, number of portables, square footage of classrooms, and information about non-classroom spaces such as athletic fields, multipurpose rooms, and cafeterias. 


Facility assessment includes evaluations of building systems (e.g., HVAC, roofs, electrical, plumbing) and identifies areas for improvement. Key difference between 2 and 3: the plan uses an assessment tool more comprehensive than the Facility Inspection Tool (FIT). 


May include reference to the impact of facility condition on learning environments (e.g., air quality, thermal comfort, accessibility).
	4 -📘
	Significant detail: Presents a comprehensive, district-wide inventory that includes detail about school buildings and classrooms, and other facilities (e.g. athletics, food service, multipurpose rooms, cafeterias). Must include square footage and detailed site plans.


Provides a robust condition assessment across all major systems (HVAC, plumbing, roofs, electrical, lighting, technology infrastructure, accessibility features). Uses multiple data sources for evaluating facility condition, such as facility audits, engineering assessments, or real-time system monitoring. 


Connects condition data to student outcomes and instructional priorities. For example mentions the importance of HVAC to indoor air quality and thermal comfort, lighting for learning environments. 


Key difference between 3 and 4: connects the facility assessment to long-term planning. The plan  identifies both immediate needs (deferred maintenance, safety issues) and long-term lifecycle considerations, and integrates both short- and long-term needs into capital planning.

2. Long-term planning for enrollment and capacity utilization


Planning for enrollment
	The FMP should include an analysis of enrollment projections, district demographics and projected changes, and incorporate those into long-range planning. These projections range from simple extrapolation of past trends to complex analyses using external consultants and demographic data. The plan should integrate this data into plans for how spaces will be used in the future, and prioritizing any site upgrades, consolidation, or other changes in use. 


Note that whether a district projects declining v. growing enrollment will shape the specific terms and types of action items that they include.
	1 -📕
	No mention or cursory mention: No discussion of enrollment trends or projections. May include a simple 3-5 year projection number but does not provide any detail. For example, does not discuss demographic changes or analysis.


No reference to whether schools are operating at capacity (e.g. number of students per school and reference to underenrollment or overenrollment).
	2 - 📙
	Limited description: Provides short-term enrollment projections (e.g., 3–5 years), may include limited discussion such as why enrollment is increasing or decreasing.


Mentions capacity utilization at least superficially (e.g. says that “some schools under-enrolled”) but does not provide details on number of students and classroom capacity or details on how space is over- or under-utilized. 


Does not connect capacity issues to projects or actions—for example the need to build new facilities or repurpose and consolidate facilities.


If the plan projects declining enrollment, there is no discussion of how space will be adapted (e.g. school closure, consolidation, or other alternative uses of space).
	3 -📗
	Sufficient detail: Provides detailed enrollment projections over a longer-term horizon (e.g., beyond 5 years), with explanation of methods or assumptions.


Identifies sites that are under- or over-capacity and discusses potential actions for addressing them.


Acknowledges long-term implications such as school closure, consolidation, new school construction, or program relocation.


Mentions alternative or flexible uses of facilities (e.g., community use, early learning, adult education), though strategies are not fully developed.
	4 -📘
	Significant detail: Provides a comprehensive enrollment analysis that describes the methodology used (e.g. demographic trends, housing patterns, or other community data).


Includes detailed capacity utilization data (by site, classroom, grade) that includes square footage, number of students, 


Connects capacity utilization analysis to specific facility actions.


Key difference between 3 and 4: clearly links enrollment projections to long-term facility needs, with explicit strategies for closure, consolidation, boundary changes, or reconfiguration.


May propose innovative or adaptive uses of space (e.g., wellness hubs, community partnerships, adult education, workforce housing).

3. Educational alignment
Educational alignment / specification
	A good FMP should be organized around the district’s educational mission and goals, and should explicitly discuss how facility use aligns with educational needs, plans, and goals. 


Some plans will include a section on “educational specifications”. Such a section typically outlines how different types of classrooms should be set up for different educational purposes: e.g. laboratories, hands-on learning, outdoor learning, STEM.  
	1 -📕
	No mention or minimal reference: Makes no or minimal mention of educational goals in framing the plan. 


Does not reference educational specifications in site plans, facility assessments, or facility projects.


Does not connect facility projects with instructional needs or educational programs.
	2 - 📙
	Limited description: References educational goals, but only in broad or generic terms.


Key difference between 1 and 2: Mentions educational specifications at a basic level (e.g., minimum classroom size, code compliance) but without details.


May include brief references to educational programs (e.g., “CTE classrooms,” “TK space”) but does not connect them to projects or strategies.


Does not describe facility requirements for specific programs (CTE, TK, special education, group learning, technology).
	3 -📗
	Sufficient detail: Includes a section on the district’s educational mission or goals and relates them to facilities (could be called educational specifications section).


Key difference between 2 and 3: Provides detailed educational specifications beyond minimum requirements. This could include discussion of STEM labs, flexible classrooms, collaborative spaces, or specialized furniture.


Connects facility projects to specific educational programs or goals (e.g., STEM classrooms, project-based learning spaces, special education facilities).


Mentions potential programmatic shifts (e.g., technology integration, early learning expansion), but coverage is partial or uneven.
	4 -📘
	Significant detail: ​​Positions educational alignment as a core organizing principle of the facilities master plan.


Demonstrates clear integration between district mission/vision, instructional priorities, and facilities planning — showing facilities as an active driver of educational improvement.


Provides a comprehensive analysis of how facilities support or fail to support multiple programs (TK, CTE, special education, collaborative learning, technology, other district priorities).


Key difference between 3 and 4: Connects facility projects and strategies directly to educational goals or specifications, so that projects are responding to educational needs (e.g., technology-rich environments, CTE labs, inclusive special education design).

4. Outdoor Spaces and Campus Greening


Outdoor Spaces and Campus Greening
	Schoolyard greening refers to the process of transforming school grounds into environmentally sustainable, park-like green spaces that can include features like trees, gardens, lawns, and permeable surfaces. 


Most FMPs will have site maps of their schools that indicate outdoor space. We are interested in how well the plan outlines these assets, and whether the plan includes discussion and strategy for expanding availability of green spaces to students. 


Some plans may also include reference to “outdoor learning”—using outdoor spaces as a learning tool or having outdoor classrooms.
	1 -📕
	No mention or minimal reference: Does not reference outdoor spaces or schoolyard greening in facilities planning.


Site maps show outdoor space only as playgrounds, athletic facilities, or parking, without mention of shade or green space.


No mention of the environmental or health benefits of outdoor space. No mention of outdoor learning.
	2 - 📙
	Limited description: Includes some reference to outdoor space (fields, playgrounds, landscaping) but provides minimal detail.


Mentions greening, shade, or outdoor learning only in passing.


Does not include data or analysis of outdoor spaces in relation to greening or shade (e.g., surface types, canopy cover, building-to-ground ratios).


Does not address student accessibility to outdoor spaces or connect green space to health or wellness.
	3 -📗
	Sufficient detail: 
Key difference between 2 and 3: provides data or analysis of outdoor spaces (e.g., surface types, canopy cover, building-to-ground ratios) with reference to greenery. 


Key difference between 2 and 3: Identifies strategies for improvement (shade structures, gardens, sustainable landscaping), though not districtwide or fully detailed.


May mention connections between greening and wellness, such as outdoor learning (e.g., outdoor classrooms, student health), but only at certain sites.
	4 -📘
	Significant Detail: Positions outdoor greening and climate-adapted design as a central element of the facilities plan.


Key difference between 3 and 4: provides districtwide data (e.g., square footage of green surfaces, description of surface types such as grass versus asphalt, data on shade coverage of outdoor spaces, or discussion of stormwater management).


Key difference between 3 and 4: provides details on strategies for campus greening (expanding tree cover, permeable surfaces, outdoor classrooms, rain gardens, bioswales, access to natural spaces).


May explicitly frame shade and green space as climate resilience strategies (heat mitigation, urban heat island reduction).


References outdoor learning, environmental literacy, and community benefits of greening projects, showing a holistic approach.

5. Climate Risk and Mitigation


Climate Risk and Mitigation
	One of the growing facilities challenges facing California schools is a disruption driven by changes in our climate and increasingly frequent and extreme climate events. In this climate era, it is critical that school facilities master plans (FMPs) address the impacts of climate change on school facilities (buildings and grounds). A few ways that FMPs might do this would be to include the following: 
* Climate Impacts Risk Assessment: Conducting a climate risk assessment to assess how climate impacts may affect school facilities. Climate impacts will vary by location and biome but may include some of the following: temperature extremes (heat and freeze), drought, floods and storms, wind, wildfires, rising sea levels, public power shut-offs, etc. A thorough assessment would include data on future projections of frequency or intensity, and will help identify vulnerable areas and support prioritizing actions to reduce exposure to these risks. 
* Climate Resilient Strategies: FMPs should provide strategies for addressing climate risks and mitigating impacts on facilities and operations. Strategies will vary by location and biome but may include some of the following: ex: shade (structures and tree planting), cool roofs, air conditioning, air filtration, stormwater management and rainwater catchment, energy resilience through microgrids, defensible space, sea walls, etc. 
* Climate Ready/Resilient Theme and Mindset: Ideally, the FMP will have climate as a central theme of the plan, both in assessment and in planning and design. This will demonstrate that a school district understands the key role that climate impacts play in the safety and health of facilities and operations. 
	0 
	No Mention of any climate risks or climate readiness. Note, they may reference emergency preparedness but not in relation to climate change.
	1 -📕
	Minimal: Acknowledges climate change impact risks / hazards (see example risks above - will vary by region) broadly, but does not provide details such as expected frequency or significance. May also reference strategies (see examples strategies above - will vary by region), but not necessarily in relation to a detailed assessment of a specific climate risk. 
	2 - 📙
	FMPs will have ONE of the following 


A) Plan addresses at least one climate impact and resilient strategy in some detail: 
* Addresses one climate impact/risk with some detail. Meaning they describe the climate impact and which aspect of facilities (buildings and grounds) are most vulnerable. 
* Provides strategies that they will use to mitigate this climate risk. 


Note, this plan might provide more detailed data or images as part of the assessment or strategy. 


OR


B) Climate Readiness / Resilience as an Assessment or Design Priority: The plan shows evidence that climate readiness / resilience is a priority for the district and is a consideration or theme of the plan, even if they do not go in-depth on specific risks or strategies across the district or for each site. 
	3 -📗
	FMPs will include BOTH of the following: 


A) Plan addresses one or more climate impacts and resilience strategies in some detail: 
* Addresses one or more climate impact/risks with some detail (see example risks above - will vary by region). Meaning they describe the climate impacts and which aspect of facilities (buildings and grounds) are most vulnerable. 
* Provides strategies (see example strategies above - will vary by region) that they will use to mitigate noted climate risk. 


Note, this level might provide more detailed data or images as part of the assessment or strategy, or it might be that they are acknowledging more climate risks. 


B) Climate Readiness / Resilience as an Assessment or Design Priority: The plan centers climate readiness / resilience as a priority for the district and is a theme of the plan in the introduction and in sections addressing projects and/or long-range planning. 


	4 -📘
	To achieve at this level, climate readiness / resilience must be a central priority for the assessment and planning process (included in the introduction, facilities overview and assessment, site-level projects, and long-range planning).


The plan should also include a comprehensive climate risks assessment for two or more climate impacts, and strategies for mitigating these risks and building long-term climate resilience that are specific to the climate impacts/risks that have been assessed.


Note to achieve at this level, the assessment and strategies should be across the district, and depending on the size of the district may also include details for individual sites.


	Model Version
	The District has conducted a comprehensive climate risk assessment to evaluate vulnerabilities to a wide range of climate impacts. Climate impacts will vary by location and biome but may include some of the following: temperature extremes (heat and freeze), drought, floods and storms, wildfires, rising sea levels, public power shut-offs, etc. A thorough assessment would include data on future projections of frequency or intensity, and will help identify vulnerable areas and support prioritizing actions to reduce exposure to these risks. 


Strategies for mitigating and addressing these climate risks may be implemented at individual sites or district-wide. Example strategies may or may not include: 
* Expanding shaded areas, reflective surfaces, and weatherization strategies to protect students and staff during heat waves and cold snaps.
* Upgrading drainage, detention basins, and bioswales to reduce flood risks from heavy storms.
* Reinforcing building envelopes and roofs to withstand high-wind events and protect critical systems.
* Installing advanced HVAC and filtration systems to maintain safe indoor air quality during wildfire smoke and other poor air quality events.
* Incorporating sea level rise adaptation strategies into coastal campus planning, including elevation, setbacks, and protective infrastructure.*
* Incorporating snow-load design and snow-melt management in regions affected by winter storms.*


Through this Facilities Master Plan establishes climate readiness and resilience as a foundational strategy for all campuses. 


By aligning with state adaptation guidance and climate threat assessments, the District demonstrates its commitment to proactive, future-ready planning.


These measures are designed not only to safeguard students and staff, but also to ensure continuity of learning and to allow schools to function as resilience hubs for their communities.

6. Energy efficiency and resilience


Energy Efficiency and Resilience
	As California faces an increasing number of climate-related disruptions—extreme heat, wildfires, smoke, and power outages—schools must modernize facilities to ensure safety, continuity of learning, and alignment with state climate goals. Energy resilience, electrification, and decarbonization are central to this effort. Energy resilience refers to a school's ability to maintain power during disruptions through on-site systems like solar, batteries, and microgrids. Electrification replaces fossil fuel systems with electric alternatives (ex: heat pumps, electric water heaters, and induction stoves) and improves indoor air quality, while decarbonization reduces greenhouse gas emissions through energy efficiency and clean energy use. Together, these strategies enable schools to operate safely during emergencies, lower energy costs, reduce pollution, and serve as resilience hubs for communities. 


The state of California mandates (Title 24: Part 6) that all new commercial buildings be Zero Net Energy (ZNE), and energy usage among existing buildings be halved by 2030. See Division of the State Architects (DSA) Sustainability Review Program, and See DSA Getting to Net Zero Over Time roadmap and toolkit.
	1 -📕
	No Mention or minimal reference: No mention of energy efficiency, electrification, decarbonization, or energy resilience.


No mention of energy-related climate impacts (e.g., power outages, extreme heat, wildfire smoke).


No reference to state requirements (Title 24, Zero Net Energy goals, DSA guidance).


No data on energy use. 
	2 - 📙
	Limited description: Key difference between 1 and 2: Mentions energy efficiency and may reference specific projects to increase energy efficiency (e.g., solar panels, LED lighting, HVAC upgrades). 


Lacks energy data or analysis of energy use. 


No comprehensive discussion of energy resilience, electrification, or decarbonization.


No reference to state requirements (Title 24, Zero Net Energy goals, DSA guidance).
	3 -📗
	Sufficient detail: May provide data on energy use. 


Like a 2-level plan, must include some energy efficiency strategies such as solar, LED lighting, HVAC upgrades, electrification of specific systems, or renewable energy. 


Key difference between 2 and 3: Discusses energy resilience as a goal.


Key difference between 2 and 3: References either Zero net energy or Title 24. 


May identify energy resilience (e.g., backup generators, solar + battery) as a goal, but does not present comprehensive strategies.
	4 -📘
	Significant detail: Key difference between 3 and 4: Positions energy resilience / energy efficiency (which may also reference electrification and decarbonization) as a core part of the facilities plan.


Key difference between 3 and 4: provides data on energy use (baselines, targets, costs) and strategies to reduce energy consumption and operating costs.


Must reference state requirements (e.g. Title 24 and Zero Net Energy goals).


May have a plan for electrification (heating, cooling, water heating, kitchens), renewable integration, and resilience strategies (e.g., solar + storage, microgrids). 


May include discussion of how improved energy systems will reduce costs, emissions, and pollution. 


May connect energy resilience to climate-related disruptions.
	Model Language
	Energy Resilience, Electrification, and Decarbonization Strategy
The District is committed to achieving carbon neutrality by 2035 through comprehensive energy resilience, electrification, and decarbonization measures. Current energy use across all facilities has been benchmarked using 2022–2023 utility data, which shows district-wide consumption of 24 million kWh of electricity and 1.2 million therms of natural gas annually.


To meet California Title 24 requirements and align with the Division of the State Architect’s (DSA) Sustainability Review Program, all new construction and major modernizations will achieve Zero Net Energy (ZNE) performance. Existing facilities will undergo phased retrofits, targeting a 50% reduction in natural gas consumption by 2030, in alignment with the state’s Getting to Net Zero Over Time roadmap.


Planned strategies include electrification of all boilers, HVAC systems, and food service equipment, with heat pump technology prioritized for space conditioning and water heating. The District will also expand its renewable portfolio through onsite solar photovoltaic arrays, battery energy storage systems, and campus-level microgrids to provide critical backup power during outages. These systems will allow schools to function as resilience hubs for their communities during climate-related emergencies such as heat waves, wildfires, and Public Safety Power Shutoffs.


This integrated approach will reduce greenhouse gas emissions, lower operating costs, and improve indoor air quality for students and staff. By embedding energy resilience and sustainability into the foundation of our facilities planning, the District will ensure safe, healthy, and future-ready schools for decades to come.

7. Financial plan and capital strategy


Financial plan and capital strategy
	A FMP should include a detailed plan for raising and spending funds to improve district facilities. This includes funding for needed maintenance and improvements (i.e. to address deficiencies identified in the facility assessment) as well as funding for large capital projects such as new schools and upgrades. 


Plans should be evaluated based on the level of detail in their financial planning, whether it gives the district sufficient guidance to move forward with implementation, and whether it provides a strategy for prioritizing projects. 
	1 -📕
	No mention or minimal reference: Does not include discussion of funding sources that will be used for facilities improvements (e.g., bonds, developer fees, state grants).


May include an overall cost of the facilities master plan or costs of some components, but does not include cost estimates for most projects.


No discussion of how projects will be prioritized.


No mention of a capital improvement plan or financial framework
	2 - 📙
	Limited description: Provides cost estimates for projects but does not include details of cost estimating methods.


Key difference between 1 and 2: mentions some funding sources (e.g., local bonds, state grants) but does not include an overall financial budget or longer-term financial plan.


May list projects in order but does not explain the criteria for prioritization.


Lacks discussion of financial context, risks, maintenance/operating costs, or alternative financing options.
	3 -📗
	Sufficient detail: Key difference between 2 and 3: Provides itemized or category-level cost estimates with some indication of estimating methods.


Key difference between 2 and 3: Describes a prioritization framework (e.g., safety first, modernization second, expansion third).


Key difference between 2 and 3: Identifies likely funding sources (local bonds, developer fees, state programs, partnerships) and outlines strategies for accessing them.


May mention maintenance/operating costs or long-term sustainability, but not comprehensively.
	4 -📘
	Significant detail: Presents a comprehensive capital strategy that includes detailed cost estimates, prioritization of projects, and detailed funding strategies.


Key difference between 3 and 4: Provides detailed cost estimates across all projects. References a method for cost estimation including escalation assumptions and contingencies. 


Uses a clear, criteria-based prioritization process (e.g., safety, equity, program alignment, lifecycle cost, climate resilience) to phase projects over time.


Key difference between 3 and 4: Outlines multiple funding strategies, including bonds, state facilities program applications, developer impact fees, and alternative financing.


Considers long-term financial sustainability, including maintenance/operations costs, debt capacity, depreciation, and bond timing.


Demonstrates alignment between financial capacity, district educational priorities, and facility needs, showing a fiscally realistic and strategic path forward.
